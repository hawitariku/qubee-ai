History of the Afaan Oromo Language and Its Writing System

June 2026 · 10 min read · Qubeessaa AI Blog
← Back to Blog

Afaan Oromo is the language of the Oromo people, the largest single ethnic group in Ethiopia and one of the major ethnic communities in Kenya. With over 40 million speakers, it is the most widely spoken Cushitic language in the world and the fourth most widely spoken language in Africa. Yet for much of modern history, Afaan Oromo was suppressed, banned from formal use, and denied a standardized writing system. This is the story of that language — how it survived, how it was finally given a script of its own, and where it stands today.

Origins: A Cushitic Language with Ancient Roots

Afaan Oromo belongs to the Cushitic branch of the Afroasiatic language family, which also includes Somali, Hadiyya, Sidama, and many other languages spoken across the Horn of Africa and beyond. The Oromo people have inhabited the regions of modern-day Ethiopia and northern Kenya for many centuries, developing a rich oral culture, complex governance systems (the Gadaa system), and a distinctive worldview expressed entirely through spoken language.

For most of its existence, Afaan Oromo was an oral language. Knowledge, history, poetry, proverbs, and law were all passed down verbally across generations. The Oromo developed an extraordinarily rich tradition of oral literature — epic songs, praise poetry (geerrarsa), and historical narratives — without any written script. This oral tradition was so robust that vast amounts of cultural knowledge were preserved and transmitted for centuries without any written form.

Early Writing Attempts: The Sabean and Ethiopic Scripts

The earliest attempts to write Afaan Oromo used the Ethiopic script — a writing system derived from ancient Ge'ez and used for Amharic and Tigrinya. Christian missionaries and later Ethiopian scholars in the 19th and early 20th centuries occasionally wrote Oromo texts in the Ethiopic script. However, the results were deeply imperfect. The Ethiopic script was designed to represent the sounds of Ge'ez, Amharic, and Tigrinya — all Semitic languages with very different sound systems from Afaan Oromo.

Afaan Oromo has several sounds — including the implosive consonant dh, the uvular consonant q, the emphatic x, and phonemically distinct vowel lengths — that simply have no equivalents in the Ethiopic script. Words written in Ethiopic for Afaan Oromo were ambiguous, inconsistent, and difficult for Oromo speakers to read without already knowing the word. The script was a poor fit.

Colonial Period and Language Suppression

The late 19th and early 20th centuries were particularly difficult for Afaan Oromo as a language. Following the expansion of the Ethiopian empire under Emperor Menelik II in the 1880s–1890s, the Oromo territories were incorporated into the Ethiopian state. Amharic was declared the sole official language of Ethiopia, and Afaan Oromo was gradually excluded from education, courts, media, and government.

By the mid-20th century, children in Oromo regions were educated exclusively in Amharic. Oromo-language books, newspapers, and publications were severely restricted. The language that more than a third of Ethiopians spoke as their first language had no official status and no standardized way to be written. This situation persisted through the reign of Haile Selassie and into the Derg military regime (1974–1991).

Historical note: The Oromo Liberation Front (OLF), founded in 1973, made Afaan Oromo language rights a central part of its political program. The development of Qubee was linked to this broader movement for Oromo cultural and political recognition.

The Development of Qubee

The modern Qubee alphabet was developed through the work of Oromo linguists, scholars, and activists beginning in the 1970s. The most significant figure in this effort was Dr. Tilahun Gamta, an Oromo linguist who developed the Latin-based orthography that would become standard Qubee. Working with other scholars, Dr. Gamta systematically analyzed the phonology of Afaan Oromo — cataloguing all its consonants, vowels, and distinctive sounds — and matched each sound to an appropriate Latin letter or combination.

The choice of the Latin alphabet was deliberate. It was phonetically flexible, widely recognized, easy to type on standard keyboards, and not associated with the scripts of Ethiopia's dominant languages. The resulting system — Qubee — could represent every phoneme in Afaan Oromo with clarity and consistency.

1991: Official Adoption of Qubee

The political landscape of Ethiopia changed dramatically in 1991 when the Derg regime fell and the Ethiopian People's Revolutionary Democratic Front (EPRDF) came to power. The new government adopted an ethnic federalism policy, recognizing regional languages and creating ethnically defined regional states. The Oromia Regional State was established, and Afaan Oromo was declared its official language.

In 1991, the Qubee alphabet was officially adopted as the standard writing system for Afaan Oromo in Ethiopia. This was a historic turning point. For the first time, Afaan Oromo had an official, standardized written form that could be used in schools, government, media, and public life. The Oromo language could now be taught, written, printed, and broadcast with a consistent, accurate script.

Afaan Oromo Today

Today, Afaan Oromo is one of Ethiopia's most prominent languages. It is the primary language of instruction in Oromia regional schools from kindergarten through high school. The Oromia Broadcasting Network broadcasts news, entertainment, and educational programming in Afaan Oromo. Several newspapers and magazines are published in the language. The internet has brought an explosion of Afaan Oromo content — social media, YouTube channels, websites, and blogs written in Qubee.

In Kenya, the Borana dialect of Oromo is spoken by hundreds of thousands of people in the northern counties. Afaan Oromo speakers also form communities in the United States, Canada, Australia, and Europe — diaspora communities that have been instrumental in developing digital tools and resources for the language.

Challenges and the Road Ahead

Despite the progress since 1991, Afaan Oromo still faces challenges as a written language. Spelling standardization remains inconsistent — different publishers, regions, and generations use slightly different conventions. Digital resources for the language are growing but still limited compared to major world languages. Natural language processing tools — spell checkers, grammar checkers, translation systems — are still in early stages compared to what is available for English, French, or even Swahili.

Conclusion

The history of Afaan Oromo and the Qubee alphabet is a story of resilience. A language that was suppressed for over a century, denied a script, and excluded from public life has emerged as a major written language with millions of literate speakers, a growing digital presence, and an increasingly robust set of educational and technological resources. The adoption of Qubee in 1991 was not just a linguistic event — it was a cultural and political milestone for the Oromo people.
